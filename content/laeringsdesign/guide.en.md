## Why asynchronous work often fails

Asynchronous work often fails when students only watch or read: without questions along the way, their minds wander more [@szpunar2013], and engagement with videos in online courses drops clearly after about six minutes [@guo2014].
A lot of new material at once creates a high cognitive load, and students then learn less [@mayer2009].
What helps is short pieces with questions that make students retrieve the material, and feedback that explains [@brame2016] [@shute2008].

## “HVL læringsdesign” in brief

HVL has developed its own model for learning design: “HVL læringsdesign”.
The model has three parts that depend on each other: kontekst (context), kunnskap (knowledge) and samskaping (co-creation).
You can use it when you plan a study programme, a course or a single learning session [@mogelvang2025].

The model builds on backward planning (baklengs planlegging) [@mogelvang2025].
You start from the learning outcomes, and plan the assessment and the activities from them [@wiggins2005].
The aim is alignment: the learning outcomes, the activities and the assessment fit together [@biggs1996].

The activities you make here belong at the model’s micro level (mikronivå), the single learning session [@mogelvang2025].
This is the work students do before, between and after the sessions (samlingar).

## Three mechanisms that help

Here are three mechanisms from research on learning.
Under each one, you can read what the research says and what it means for your activity.

### Segment and retrieve

We can only handle a little new material at a time, so material is easier to understand when it comes in short pieces that students pace themselves [@mayer2009].
Short videos also hold attention better [@guo2014], and questions between the pieces reduce mind wandering and improve learning [@szpunar2013].

Retrieving what you have learnt strengthens long-term memory more than reading it again [@roediger2006].
The effect is greatest when practice is spread over time [@cepeda2006].
Practice testing is among the learning strategies with the best documented effect [@dunlosky2013].

> - Keep videos and texts short, and split them into pieces of a few minutes.
> - Add a question after each piece, so that students have to retrieve or use what they have just seen [@brame2016].
> - Let students take the tasks again a few days later, so that they practise with gaps in between.

### Explain, don’t just mark

Being told that an answer is wrong says little about what to do differently.
Feedback that explains works better than right or wrong alone [@shute2008].
It helps most when it is about the task and the work, not the person, and tells students where they stand and what to do next [@hattie2007].
Good feedback also helps students judge their own work, and gives them a chance to get closer to the goal [@nicol2006].

> - Write feedback for every answer option, and explain why it is right or wrong.
> - When a wrong answer comes from a common misconception, name the misconception and say what holds instead.
> - Let students try again after the feedback, so that they can use it straight away.

### Practise judgement safely

Professionals often meet situations that are unclear or unique, where no rule gives the answer [@schon1983].
Judgement grows when you act, see the consequences and think about them as you go, what Schön calls reflection-in-action [@schon1983].
Van Manen writes about pedagogical tact: a sense of what the situation calls for, and the ability to act thoughtfully in the moment [@vanmanen1991].
A case with choices lets students practise such situations without anyone being harmed.
Feedback on every choice lets them learn from the path, not only from the outcome [@shute2008].

> - Base the case on a realistic situation from professional practice in your subject.
> - Give every choice feedback that explains its consequences, also when the choice is a good one.
> - Let students go back and try a decision again.
> - End with questions that make students think about their choices, rather than giving points for “right” choices.
